# Fact-Checking Pattern for Ghostwriter Terminal

> Trigger: Vinny says "fact check it," "use your browser skill to fill in the gaps," or any variant requesting verification.

## Workflow

1. **Identify every named claim** in the outline: specific people, quotes, dates, documents, numbers, URLs.
2. **Verify each with targeted searches**, not open-ended topic research. Check:
   - The vendor's own site (announcements, blog posts, system cards)
   - Reputable tech journalism (The Verge, WIRED, Reuters, WSJ, Ars Technica, CyberScoop)
   - Academic or benchmark sources (Epoch AI, arXiv)
   - Daily data files Vinny provides (IronPulse, TensorFeed, blogwatcher dumps)
3. **If a claim cannot be verified**, omit it. Do not include unverified claims unlabeled. Adjust surrounding prose if the omission breaks the argument.
4. **Add a SOURCES section** at the end of the article listing every primary source URL used. Format:
   ```
   - Publication: "Article Title" (date) - URL
   ```

5. **Pull the FULL abstract, not the outline's paraphrase.** The outline compresses a paper to two lines and drops the strongest numbers. Fetching the source page directly usually returns exact figures that make the article sharper: effect sizes, sample sizes, confidence bands, base rates, named systems. Use the real numbers the paper reports instead of the rounded version the outline kept. Every number that enters the article must be one you read on the source page, never one recalled from the outline's summary of it.
6. **Resolve prior-article cross-references before writing.** When the outline names earlier field notes, read them on disk to get their exact titles and theses. Prior PhantomByte drafts live at `/mnt/c/Users/<user>/workspace/article-drafts/ghostwriter-terminal/` as `ARTICLE <n> COMPLETE.txt` plus dated `YYYY-MM-DD-slug-draft.txt` files. Read the first lines for the real title and quote that title accurately in the new piece. Do not invent a title for a note you have not opened, and do not trust the outline's short handle for it. Quote the whole first line: a prior note's real title often carries a second sentence, and the outline's handle drops it, so the cross-reference names the full H1 rather than the fragment.
   - **The outline's own note number is an outline-internal label, not a draft filename.** An outline that cites "#100" or "#101" is not saying a file named `ARTICLE 100 COMPLETE.txt` exists; the same note may live only as a dated `YYYY-MM-DD-slug-draft.txt` with no matching `ARTICLE <n>` file. Locate prior notes by their title words (grep the real title, or list the drafts dir and read first lines), never by grepping for `ARTICLE <number>`, which returns nothing and looks like a dead end.
   - **When the outline references a prior note by paraphrase with no number (a description of its pattern rather than its title), do NOT grep the drafts dir for the outline's phrase.** The phrase is a summary, not a verbatim string, and the grep returns nothing while looking like a dead end. Instead list the drafts directory, read the first line of each candidate file, and pick the note whose real title and thesis match the description. When a more recent note sits squarely on the section being written (for example a stacking section when a note measures how stacked gates compose), cite it too; it strengthens the piece and the cross-reference stays accurate.
   - **Resolve a slug-cited reference against the live sitemap, not the outline's date.** When the outline cites a prior note by URL slug, fetch `https://articles.phantom-byte.com/sitemap.xml` with one scrapling get and grep it for the slug to get the real page and title. The slug is the reliable key; the date is not. An outline's date for a reference is routinely a day or two off from the draft's writing day, and the disk drafts are named by writing day, so locating a note by the outline's date lands on the wrong article entirely. Confirm the slug in the sitemap, then read the matching disk draft's first lines to confirm the thesis before quoting the title in the body and in the SOURCES entry. Fetching the sitemap for this is allowed; it is slug lookup, not the article browsing the style rules forbid.
   - **Append `.html` to the slug before you decide a note is dead.** articles.phantom-byte.com serves every article at `https://articles.phantom-byte.com/<slug>.html`. The bare sitemap slug returns `Fetched (404)` with a 142-byte nginx stub while the `.html` form of the same slug returns 200 with a 60 to 80 KB page, so testing the bare slug makes live notes look deleted and pushes you into substituting references that were fine. Take the slug from the sitemap, fetch `<slug>.html`, and only after that 404s treat the note as unpublishable. A trailing slash on the bare slug does not help; the extension is the requirement. The homepage confirms the convention: `scrapling extract get https://articles.phantom-byte.com/ index.html` then `grep -o 'href="[^"]*"' index.html` lists every article as a bare relative filename ending in `.html`.
   - **A sitemap entry is not proof the page resolves.** The sitemap can list a slug whose page returns `404 Not Found`, a note pulled after publish or one that only ever existed as a draft. Grep the sitemap for the slug, fetch it in its `.html` form, and read the status and byte size before you quote it in a cross-reference or a SOURCES line; a 404 stub is a few dozen bytes of nginx text. When the note really is dead it is not citable, so cross-reference the verified notes you actually opened rather than shipping a URL a reader cannot check.
   - **Read the H1 out of the saved page, not out of the sitemap.** The sitemap carries slugs only, and the `<title>` tag is an SEO rewrite that differs from the piece's own heading. `grep -o '<h1[^>]*>[^<]*' page.html` gives the real H1, and when the H1 wraps in a span (`article-title"><span>`) grep the next fragment for the second clause. Quote that whole line: the prior note's real title usually carries a subtitle the outline's handle drops, and the cross-reference names the full H1.
   - **The disk draft's title and the published H1 routinely disagree, and the live H1 wins.** Publishing rewrites the headline for search, so a draft opening on a thesis line can be live under a different name at the same slug. The draft title is only good for locating the file; the live H1 is what a reader clicks into, so the cross-reference quotes it and the SOURCES line sits beside it. Read both, and when they differ, ship the live one and do not carry the draft's wording into the new piece.
   - **A named prior note can have no article and no draft anywhere.** When the outline names a title, check the drafts directory and the live page, and if neither has it, do not invent a URL or an adjacent title to fill the slot. Resolve the cross-reference to the real notes on disk that cover the nearest territory, quote their exact H1s, and say the substitution in one line in the delivery message so Vinny knows which reference moved.
   - **Save the sitemap as `.txt`, and grep the bare URL, not the `<loc>` tag.** `extract get` refuses an `.xml` output name with `ValueError: Unknown file type`, so name the file `sitemap.txt`. The text extraction then strips the XML tags, so `grep -o "<loc>[^<]*</loc>"` returns zero matches and reads exactly like an empty sitemap. Grep the URL pattern instead (`grep -io "https://articles.phantom-byte.com/[a-z0-9-]*" sitemap.txt | sort -u`) and check that the slug you need is in that list before concluding the note does not exist.
   - **An empty sitemap stub is a failed lookup, not a missing note.** Check the file size first: a sitemap saved at a few dozen bytes answered with nothing usable, and the fix is to fall back to the drafts directory, not to conclude the note never existed.
   - **Grep a number, never a currency string.** A pattern containing `$130` is read as a backreference and grep exits with "Invalid back reference", printing no matches, which reads exactly like the note is absent. Grep `130 billion`, then `head -2` every hit: an outline's handle for a prior note ("The $130 Billion Blockade") is a paraphrase that will not appear in the file anywhere, so only the number and the first line identify it. The real title is usually the full H1 plus a colon and a second clause, and that whole line is what the cross-reference in the new piece must quote.

## Fetching Sources with the Scrapling CLI

Use the `scrapling` CLI, not a Python import. The Python module is frequently not importable from the default interpreter even when scrapling is installed, so `from scrapling.fetchers import ...` can fail while the CLI works fine. Resolve to the CLI:

```bash
SCRATCH=$(mktemp -d /tmp/hermes-cron-XXXXXX)
scrapling extract get "https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07359" "$SCRATCH/paper.txt"
```

Notes that make this reliable:
- `extract get` handles plain pages. Use `extract fetch` for Cloudflare, Turnstile, or JS-rendered pages; `fetch` is the StealthyFetcher in scrapling 0.5, and the old `stealthy-fetch` subcommand was removed so it fails if called.
- It writes whatever the server returns to the file you name and prints `Fetched (NNN)` plus the saved path. Read the STATUS CODE on that line, not just the "saved" message: a 404 still logs `Content successfully saved` and drops a small stub (nav chrome plus a "404 - Page not found" line, a few hundred bytes) where the article should be. Confirm the status is 200 and the saved file is article-sized before quoting it.
- **Never guess the URL slug.** When the outline names a publication and date but no URL, run one search for the headline and fetch the canonical URL the search returns; a hand-built slug 404s while looking like a successful save. This is the fastest fix when a fetch lands 404. When the search returns unrelated results, read the slug out of the publication's own index instead of guessing again: save the index as `.html` (`scrapling extract get https://huggingface.co/blog "$SCRATCH/index.html"`) and grep `href="/blog/[a-z0-9._-]*"`. The text extraction of the same page keeps the post titles but strips the links, so a `.txt` save cannot resolve a slug and looks like the post does not exist.
- **An outline that names a paper but no arXiv ID: one search URL returns the ID.** `scrapling extract get "https://arxiv.org/search/?query=<Title>&searchtype=all"` lists the identifier, the page and figure count, the submission date, and the author list with its et al. count, which is everything the SOURCES line needs. Then fetch `/abs/<id>` for the abstract and `/html/<id>v1` for the body. Do not construct an abs URL from a guessed identifier; arXiv IDs are not derivable from a title, and a wrong one 404s into a small stub.
- It fetches several URLs back to back in one call, so batch every source for the article into a single loop and read the outputs after.
- **NBER and SSRN working papers: fetch the abstract page, never the PDF.** `https://www.nber.org/papers/w<number>` returns 200 on a plain `extract get` and carries the full abstract with the effect sizes and p-values, the enrolled and completed sample counts, the authors, and the JEL codes, which is everything the body and the SOURCES line need. A direct `.pdf` URL from a mirror (a university center's copy of the same paper, for example) extracts as tens of thousands of lines of raw PDF objects (`7338:91 0 obj`, xref tables) and greps as empty, which reads exactly like a failed fetch; drop it and go to the abstract page. An outline's paraphrase of an economics paper keeps the direction and drops the standard deviations, so take every number off the abstract page and let the abstract's own wording set the claim.
- **arXiv: fetch `https://arxiv.org/abs/<id>` directly.** The abstract page renders the full abstract with no expansion click needed, and it also carries the authors, submission date, and comments line, which is what the SOURCES section needs. When the article needs method detail rather than a claim, follow it with `https://arxiv.org/html/<id>v1`, which `extract get` renders as the full paper body: that is where the numbers an implementation section needs live, such as the branch count, the fan-out cadence and its trigger points, the per-branch budget fraction, the held-out agreement rate of the method's own measurements, and the exact model and harness versions. Fetch both pages, the abstract page for the SOURCES line and the HTML page for the mechanics. A detection, benchmark, or evaluation abstract often carries no numbers at all, only the claim that the method works, so the HTML page is mandatory rather than optional whenever a figure is going into the article. A 78KB HTML fetch is normal and greps cleanly for the constants.
- **TechCrunch and other adblocker-walled news sites:** a direct `extract get` on the article URL can still return the full body when the overlay blocks a browser session. Check the saved file's size before assuming it failed. TechCrunch also answers `extract get` with a 301 and only resolves on `extract fetch`, which follows the redirect to the trailing-slash URL and returns 200.
- **Data Center Dynamics 403s the plain fetcher.** `datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/<slug>` returns `Fetched (403)` on `extract get` and writes a 16-byte stub, then returns `Fetched (200)` with the full article body on `extract fetch`. This is the primary source for a large share of AI infrastructure financing and data center capacity stories, so reach for `extract fetch` on the first attempt rather than treating the 403 as a dead link.
- **MarkTechPost 403s `extract get` on every article URL and serves the full body on `extract fetch`.** The plain fetch writes a 16-byte stub that greps as empty, which reads like the article is gone; the `fetch` retry lands `Fetched (200)` with 10 to 30 KB of body including the results tables and the install or config section. Most AI-release stories in the daily data files cite MarkTechPost, so treat the 403 as expected rather than as a dead link. Its article pages also carry the per-model results tables the data-file summary compressed into one line, which is where the numbers the outline dropped live.
- **openai.com pages 403 on `extract get`, then succeed on `extract fetch` with a trailing slash.** A plain `extract get` on an `openai.com/index/<slug>` page returns `Fetched (403)` and writes a 0-byte file. Re-fetch the same page with `extract fetch`; it follows the 308 redirect and lands `Fetched (200)` once the URL resolves with a trailing slash (`https://openai.com/index/<slug>/`), saving the full page text including the study numbers, named people, and quotes. The same `extract fetch` retry works on any vendor or publisher page that 403s the plain fetcher, including a sponsored vendor analysis hosted on a tech blog, where a plain `extract get` returns `Fetched (403)` and writes a 16-byte stub. Check the saved byte size before reading, and do not treat a 403 as proof the article is gone.
- **GitHub pull request pages fetch cleanly with `extract get`, and the PR body is a citable primary source.** `https://github.com/<org>/<repo>/pull/<n>` returns the full description written by the PR author, which often carries the method, the motivation, and specifics no vendor summary repeats (hashing choice and why, the capture ladder, cost controls, measured artifacts). The page header also gives the author handle, the open date, and whether the PR is still a draft, which is exactly what the SOURCES line needs. Fetch it alongside the vendor writeup; do not cite the vendor summary for something the PR states more precisely.
- **`extract get` only writes `.md`, `.html`, or `.txt` filenames.** Naming the output `pr.json` aborts the write after the fetch with `ValueError: Unknown file type: filename must end with '.md', '.html', or '.txt'`. If you need an API response, name the output `.txt`, or skip the API and keep the HTML page.
- **A grep that prints nothing has not proved absence until the pattern compiled.** A malformed bracket expression, such as an escaped hyphen inside a nested range, makes grep fail with "Invalid range end" on stderr while the stdout you read shows zero matches, which scrolls past identically to a page that genuinely lacks the term. Keep the class simple (`[A-Za-z0-9.-]`), test the pattern against one line you know matches before drawing a conclusion, and on a rendered paper body prefer converting the HTML to text in Python once and grepping the plain text.
- **One search finds the canonical URL, then the fetch is the source.** A search result snippet is a pointer, never evidence. Take the URL the search returns, preferring the vendor's own domain, the SEC filing or press-release copy, or the named analyst note, fetch that page with scrapling, and quote only what the fetched page says. The same search is the fastest way to settle a figure the outline carries but no data file corroborates, such as a capacity or contract-term number: if the primary page states it, cite it; if only aggregators repeat it, drop it.

## Pitfalls

- **An outline's relative date is a claim, not a fact.** Phrases like "out yesterday," "this week," and "just dropped" are written when the outline is drafted and are routinely wrong by the time the piece is filed. Every arXiv abstract page prints the real submission date and most vendor pages carry a publish date, so read that date and write the fact from it. When the outline's relative date is off, put the actual date in the body ("posted Monday") instead of inheriting the phrase, and name the correction in the delivery note. **An outline's own DATE field is the drafting date, not the source's date.** It records the day Vinny wrote the outline, and an outline drafted later than the news will claim the news broke on its own date, or say several sources "landed the same day" when they share an earlier submission date on arXiv. Read each primary page's date and write those; when the outline's framing depends on the sources being simultaneous, check that they actually are.
- **GitHub stats are live, not static.** Star counts, forks, issues, and commit numbers change by the hour. The outline's numbers are almost always stale. Always pull fresh counts from the GitHub repository page at verification time and use those in the article. Do not propagate stale outline numbers.
- **raw.githubusercontent.com can fail silently.** Browser navigation to `raw.githubusercontent.com` URLs may return an empty or truncated snapshot even when the page loads. If raw content is empty, use the GitHub web UI snapshot or the daily data files as backup. Do not spend more than one attempt on raw GitHub content.
- **Model hardware and memory claims: get real byte counts from the Hugging Face API, do not guess VRAM.** Vendor model cards usually state parameter counts and nothing about memory. Append `?blobs=true` to the model API URL to get the exact size of every weight file in the repo:
  ```bash
  curl -s "https://huggingface.co/api/models/<org>/<model>?blobs=true" | python3 -c "import sys,json; d=json.load(sys.stdin); [print(s['rfilename'], round(s['size']/1024/1024,1),'MB') for s in d['siblings'] if s.get('size')]; print('total MB', round(d['usedStorage']/1024/1024,1))"
  ```
  The plain `https://huggingface.co/api/models/<org>/<model>` response does NOT include sizes (both curl and scrapling return an empty or size-less body for it, so use the `?blobs=true` variant). This gives a defensible number for an article, e.g. "the small classifier ships at about 105 MB," and it lets you tell a reader whether the model fits consumer hardware without inventing a VRAM figure. `usedStorage` in the same response gives the whole-repo total. Verified 2026-09-30 against `nvidia/Kumo-Tabular` (small classifier 104.8 MB, large classifier 815.2 MB, repo total 2325.0 MB).
- **Hugging Face carries two different sources, and the hardware floor lives in a different one than the numbers.** The model card README's serving or deployment section is where an outline's minimum-hardware claim actually lives (a table listing, for example, "FP8: single node on 4 B300 GPUs" and "one H200 node, 8 GPUs"), while the HF blog post at `huggingface.co/blog/<org>/<slug>` carries the vendor's own parameter counts, context length, quantization sizes, and throughput. Fetch both: the model card for the floor and the serving minimum, the blog post for the vendor numbers. Never restate an outline's "minimum N GPUs" or "N billion parameters" line without reading the model card it came from.
- **Vendor pricing pages are the only source for a rate and its effective date.** Google's `ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing` returns 200 on a plain `extract get` and prints the introductory rate, the standard rate, the cached-input rate, and the exact switch date in one table. Read the table rather than a blog summary of it, because aggregators disagree on when the higher rate starts. When a pricing page genuinely blocks bots, OpenAI's `/api/pricing` and its kin often do, try the `developers.` or `platform.` subdomain before anything else. If every vendor path fails, use a dated trade report that names the vendor page, and never fall back to the outline's rate or effective date: inheriting the outline's number is how a wrong forward date ships.
- **A published run cost has to reconcile from tokens before it enters the article.** Labs publish a total and a token breakdown in the same paper, so rebuild the total from the parts: cached input at the cache rate, uncached input at the full input rate, and output at the output rate, including thinking tokens which usually bill as output. A rebuild that lands within a few percent of the published figure is verified arithmetic and can be stated as such; a rebuild that is off by a wide margin means the published total covers a different scope than the token counts, which is the common case when a session cost includes a monitoring agent and post-milestone activity. Say which scope each number belongs to before computing a per-unit rate. Do not present the rebuild as the lab's own measurement, and do not present a per-unit rate the lab never published without calling it back-of-the-envelope.
- **A headline cost and its action counts can cover different windows.** A session total often keeps running after the milestone that the program, message, and failure counts stop at, so dividing the full total by those counts overstates cost per unit. State both denominators or use the up-to-the-milestone figure for per-unit work and label it.
- **Don't trust single-source reporting** for politically charged claims (e.g., who reported whom to the government). Cross-reference with at least two independent outlets.
- **Don't chase open-ended research.** If the outline says "FrontierMath," verify FrontierMath. Don't start researching Anthropic's entire history.
- **Daily data files are primary sources.** IronPulse and TensorFeed dumps often contain the earliest reporting on a story. Check them before web search.
- **The vendor page beats the data-file summary on specifics.** A blogwatcher/IronPulse paraphrase rounds figures and shifts dates. When the data-file summary and the vendor's own page disagree on a number, a date, or a name, cite what the vendor page says and quote the vendor page's title and date. The data-file summary is a pointer to the source, not the source.
- **Dead outline URL rescue.** When a specific URL in the outline returns 404 or fails to load, do not mark the claim unverifiable immediately. Check the daily data files (TensorFeed, blogwatcher, IronPulse), they often reproduce the same announcement with correct sourcing. If the claim itself is confirmed there, cite the daily data file as the source. Only omit the claim if it cannot be corroborated by any primary source.
- **A vendor newsroom homepage is not a source, and a document's own counts must reconcile before you quote either.** Fetch the newsroom index only to locate the specific release URL; when the release page cannot be fetched, cite the trade outlet that reported the session by name and date with no URL, never the homepage, because a link that carries none of the claim is a citation the reader cannot check and it reads as padding in the SOURCES block. The same page-count trap sits in any story built on one long document: when two sources describe it by count and by fraction, divide in code and state both figures in the body so they corroborate. "Nearly a third" beside "93 of 335 pages" agrees at 28 percent; a pair that does not divide is two different documents and one of them is wrong.
- **Vendor blog URL slug mismatch (especially NVIDIA).** Vendor announcement URLs often use long, non-obvious slugs that do not match the headline. NVIDIA, in particular, frequently uses descriptive slugs like `/blog/nvidia-agent-toolkit-open-models-tools-skills-secure-runtime-ai-agents/` rather than `/blog/agent-toolkit/`. The outline's guessed URL may 404 while the canonical URL lives in your daily data files. Always verify vendor URLs against the blogwatcher or IronPulse dump before marking them unreachable.
- **NVIDIA blog URLs are especially unstable.** Multiple instances of 404 within days of publication. Always have TensorFeed / daily data files ready as fallback.
- **Truncated arXiv abstracts in search results.** arXiv search result pages truncate abstracts with a "More" link. Clicking that link via browser tools often fails with DOM box-model errors. Fix: navigate directly to `https://arxiv.org/abs/<id>` instead. The abstract page renders the full text immediately without expansion clicks.
- **A detection figure is meaningless without its IoU threshold and its table row.** Precision and average precision in object detection work are reported at one or more overlap thresholds, usually 25 and 50 percent intersection over union, and a per-class figure sits in a different table row from the all-class mAP. An outline and a data-file summary both drop those two facts, and the omissions change the story entirely: the same model can read 72.5 percent for the target class at the loose threshold, 50.3 percent mAP across all classes at that same threshold, and 53.8 and 47.1 at the strict one. Before any percentage enters the article, open the results and ablation tables, name the threshold and the row it came from, and put the strict-threshold number beside the headline one. Never present a per-class figure as overall accuracy, and never present the loose-threshold number as the one a deployer will see. The same discipline applies to any metric with a hidden knob: recall at k, pass rate at a budget, accuracy at a quantization level.
- **A benchmark paper's main table has more than one baseline row, and an outline will merge two of them.** An outline that quotes a headline score and a mechanism-isolation gain in the same breath can attach the headline baseline to the sub-experiment, which ran on a different task subset with its own baseline. Read the table rows the numbers actually sit in before writing a sentence that ties them together, and state each gain against the baseline it was measured on. A percentage computed against the wrong row is a fabricated number even though every figure in it came off the page.
- **A paper with two interventions attaches each delta to one of them, and an outline collapses the two.** When an abstract introduces intervention A, then intervention B, then quotes a single point delta, the sentence carrying the number names the one it belongs to, and it is frequently the second. Verify which before writing: an outline that opens with A and then reports B's delta hands the reader the stronger claim on the weaker evidence, and the error propagates into the social post when the post is written from the outline's framing instead of the finished body. Write the figure against its own intervention, and where the other intervention has no published number, say it raises the effect and stop there. That sentence is honest and it is what the authors published.
- **A product category claim is verified in the docs, not the announcement.** Launch posts say what a product is positioned as; compatibility pages, architecture FAQs, and API references say what it is built from. For anything the article calls speech to speech, end to end, fused, single model, or agentic, fetch the vendor docs page as well as the announcement and quote the implementation sentence. Two vendors can ship the same architectural answer on the same day and still both be cascades under the marketing, which changes the article's thesis from "the middle step is gone" to "the middle step moved inside one vendor." When the outline's premise and the docs disagree, the docs win and the body gets rewritten around the corrected mechanism.
- **Leaderboard placement claims are checked on the leaderboard page.** A trade writeup saying a model sits third is a pointer. Fetch the benchmark site itself; its FAQ or summary text usually states the ranking in one sentence naming every model in order, which is stronger evidence than the article that repeated it. Cite that page in SOURCES beside the vendor's own numbers.
- **The outline quotes one metric from a results table and drops the one that moves the opposite way.** A training-result post headlines two numbers and its own table carries three, and the third is usually coverage. Before writing the paragraph, open the post's results table (and the paper's table or appendix caption, since a benchmark abstract often carries no figures at all and the number the outline kept can live in a figure note rather than the results section) and read every column in the row. Then put the trade in the body as the source states it: a success rate climbing while the share of tasks solved at least once slips is repeatability bought with breadth, which is a different and more useful claim than "the model got better." Name the stronger baseline that still leads on the headline number, keep both denominators, and treat the caveat as a finding rather than a weakness in the piece. An outline that omits the third column is not lying; it is compressing a table into a sentence, and the reader can only act on the shape you give them.
- **Name the exact base model and checkpoint, never "the model."** An outline routinely says a lab "ran a reinforcement-learning loop" without naming what ran, and the vendor post and the paper both name it. A reader cannot check a result without knowing the base model, and the named model plus the step or checkpoint is what makes the paragraph citable and quotable later.
- **An outline's single range can merge a family figure with one member's worst case.** When an outline states a spread like "three to seven times longer," read the source sentence before writing it: the article underneath usually reports a family range (three to four times) and then one member's outlier (Rubin possibly needing seven). Collapsing those into one span states a minimum the source never gave, and every reader who checks the source finds the piece overstating the low end. Write both parts as the source does, and name the correction in the delivery note with the publication. The same merge happens with pricing bands, capacity ranges, and benchmark spreads, so treat any outline range as two claims until the sentence proves otherwise.
- **A head-to-head comparison where one arm is modeled is not a measurement.** Vendor benchmark charts routinely pair a measured run of the new path against an estimate of the old one, and the estimate is built from assumptions (calls per request, fixed instruction tokens re-sent each turn, typical result sizes). The methodology section states those assumptions; the chart and the trade writeup under it drop them, and the outline inherits the chart. Read the methodology before writing a single number from the comparison, then in the body name which side was measured and which is the vendor's model of the alternative, with the assumption stated ("models 3 to 10 calls per request and about 28,000 tokens of instructions per request"). Lead the piece with the vendor's own framing rather than asserting the modeled number as a result, and say plainly in one sentence that the modeled side is an argument against a harness the vendor has not seen. The measured half is usually still the stronger number, so nothing is lost by being exact.
- **A net percentage can hide a component that grew.** A headline cut to a request's fixed cost is the sum of several component changes, and the vendor's own budget table usually shows one of them going the other way (tool definitions down sharply while the system prompt grows, because new guidance on images, connected apps, or personal data landed in the same release). Read the component table, not just the total, then put the growth in the body beside the cut: it tells the reader where the fat actually is and where careful teams keep spending, and it stops the piece from implying a trend that runs through every line of the prompt. When the release also switched models, say that the tokenizer changed and give the character count as the tokenizer-independent check.
- **An average saving that concentrates in a tail of tasks is an adoption condition, not a headline.** A published per-task saving is often dominated by the small fraction of tasks that make many sequential calls, and the source's own sentence says so (savings concentrating in the tasks that make six or more tool calls, for example). Find that sentence and write it as a limit on the recommendation: a workload that makes one call per turn pays sandbox overhead and inherits a new failure mode instead of banking a saving. That caveat is stronger material than the average, because it tells the reader whether the thing applies to them.
- **Never write a SOURCES line for a prior PhantomByte note before its slug is resolved in the sitemap.** The slug is a rewrite of the headline, not a transform of it, so a slug built from the title reads as plausible and 404s for every reader who clicks. Resolve the real slug out of `sitemap.xml`, fetch `<slug>.html` to confirm 200 and page size, then write the citation. A guessed slug ships inside a list of otherwise verified links, which is the one place a reader is least likely to re-check.
- **Quote a prior note's H1 in quotation marks when its title carries a period.** Cross-referencing a title like `Your Cost Curve Just Broke. Harness Efficiency Now Decides Your AI Bill` inside running prose without quote marks produces a sentence where a full stop does a comma's job and the reader loses the thread. Wrap the whole H1, both clauses, in quotation marks and let the surrounding sentence close once.
- **The "two different models" challenge.** Vinny may send back the same article under two filenames and claim two different models couldn't produce identical text. The correct response is not to argue about model behavior, it's to diff the files, show they're byte-for-byte identical, and prove via session_search that a single session produced the file. The "two models" claim is a red herring; the real issue is whether you recognize your own work and can prove it.
