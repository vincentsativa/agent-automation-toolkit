# LinkedIn Post Generator

## When to Use

Load this when Vinny asks for a LinkedIn post, "what would be good to post about on LinkedIn", "something to post on LinkedIn", or when he forwards third-party posting advice (his own AI chat, a course, a blog) and asks whether it is right. The fact-check answer has to come out the same every time, so it lives here.

Not this skill: syndicating a finished PhantomByte article (`article-syndication`), or X posts (`x-algorithm-post-optimization`).

Vinny posts on LinkedIn while job hunting. Hiring managers and technical peers are the readers. That changes the job: the post has to demonstrate senior judgment without announcing that it is a portfolio piece. A post that says "I led the migration" reads as a resume line. A post that inverts a deployment order and names the failure mode reads as the person who would have caught it.

## Inputs

- Topic source is the day's local drop, `.../<user>/OneDrive/Desktop/TODAYS DATA FILES/`. Read every file in it, not just one. **A second copy of the same folder exists at `~/Desktop/TODAYS DATA FILES`, and it is a stale subset that holds the tensorfeed reports but none of the `outlineN-*.txt` files.** List the `/mnt/c` path first and treat that one as the drop; a session that reads the home copy concludes the outlines do not exist and starts improvising a subject.
- The `outlineN-*.txt` files are the strongest pool because they already carry a thesis, verified numbers and source traceability. `tensorfeed_report_*.txt` and `ironpulse-*.txt` corroborate the figures and supply alternates.
- **Read the article file that is publishing tonight and exclude its subject entirely.** When he names an off-limits file, that file's whole theme is out, not just its headline. Pick a different outline, and do not slide into a neighbouring angle on the same subject.
- Compare the surviving outlines on transferability. An industry or physical-AI story that yields a discipline the reader can apply travels further than a finance story that reads like news commentary. The one that shows how he reasons under ambiguity wins.
- **He can name an outline by a Windows path outside the drop, usually something under `C:\Users\<user>\Downloads\`.** Translate it to `.../<user>/Downloads/...` and read it there. A named path is the whole input spec: do not go hunting in TODAYS DATA FILES for it, and do not conclude the outline is missing because it is not on the drop list.
- The subject is not always the data drop. He sometimes names his own artifact instead, a repo he just published, a script, a pipeline. Then the artifact is the source and the post has to prove its claims from it. See "When He Hands You a Repo or a Live Artifact" below.

## Post Shape

- **No required length.** A post runs as long as the argument needs and no longer. 200 to 300 words is a house starting range that has worked for one claim plus one framework. It is not a target to hit, not a spec, and not a platform recommendation. A post with a numbered sequence and a bulleted evidence list lands near 290 to 330, and a post making a single point can land at 150. Both are correct.
- Open on a specific claim with a number or a named decision in the first line. Never open on a general statement, and never open with the framing device ("here is a lesson from"). **When the story is an announcement, open on the cost to the reader and let the announcement land on the second line.** "Anthropic published a system called X" describes the news and gives a scrolling reader nothing to lose; "Your vendor can swap the weights and you would never know" names an exposure the reader is already carrying. The claim still has to be one the source supports, stays in second person, and the news arrives immediately after so the post is not a tease.
- Explain the mechanism in the middle and name the failure mode plainly: most teams skip to the last stage, the incident report blames the model, the model was fine.
- **Name the artifact's maturity stage out loud when the source is an announcement.** One sentence stating that it is a research post with code on a repo and no deployment plan reads to a hiring manager as judgment, not negativity. Summarizing the announcement is what every other poster does; stating what it cannot yet do is what the person who has shipped does.
- Give the reader something to do: a short sequence, the evidence gates, the questions to demand before advancing.
- Close on a challenge to the reader, one sentence, second person, PhantomByte manner.
- **Two to three sentences per paragraph is the default. Every sentence on its own line, all the way down, is a defect he will name.** He calls that shape breathless LinkedIn bro-etry, and he is right: a post where every sentence stands alone reads as a template instead of a person making an argument. Group the sentences that carry one idea (what the thing is, how the check works, why it runs on a closed model) into a single block, and break between ideas rather than between full stops. Keep a single-line beat for emphasis only: the maturity warning, the turn into a framework, the procurement ask, the closing question. Four or five of those in a post of this length is the ceiling, and a post made entirely of them is the failure shape. No prose block past three sentences; when one runs long, break it where the idea changes, not after every full stop. Numbered and bulleted list items stay one line each, because order and scannability are the point of a list, not rhythm.
- Close with 3 to 4 targeted hashtags on the last line, after the closing question and nothing below them. Pick tags that describe the audience the post is aimed at (a domain tag, a role tag, a technology tag), not tags that describe the news. Four is the working number; more reads as spam and fewer forfeits discovery.
- Structural cores get a list, the argument stays prose. A sequence of stages, a set of gating metrics, or a checklist of what to demand becomes a numbered list when order matters and bullets when it does not. Keep the reasoning in prose around the list. What to avoid is a list buried inside an argumentative paragraph; the visible skeleton is what makes the post skimmable on mobile, and he asks for it on revision when it is missing.
- Name the actor, never "the thing". Concrete nouns carry the authority: the agent, the harness, the incident report, third party APIs. "Wire the thing into production" reads like a summary of someone else's work; "wire the agent into production" reads like the person who has done it.
- No em dashes, no en dashes, no hyphens standing in for dashes. No markdown headers in the post.
- Follow `phantom-byte-writing-style` for voice. It governs every word.

## Length: What Is and Is Not True

**Nothing dictates the length, not LinkedIn and not this skill.** The 200 to 300 word range is a house starting point for a post that carries one claim and one framework. It is not a rule, not a quality bar, and not something to report as met or missed. Length follows the content: add a numbered sequence and a bulleted evidence list and the draft runs longer, cut to a single point and it runs shorter. The only real ceiling is 3,000 characters.

Never repeat the claim that LinkedIn recommends 200 to 300 words. There is no such recommendation, and saying there is puts a fabricated authority in his mouth in front of hiring managers.

- The only length figure LinkedIn publishes is the 3,000 character cap, with text folding behind See More at roughly 140 characters. Lead with the sentence that has to survive the fold.
- Ranking is LLM and GPU based, confirmed on LinkedIn's own engineering blog on March 12, 2026, scored on what a post is about plus how members interact with it over time. No word count target is published anywhere.
- The 1,900 to 2,000 word figure that circulates as "ideal LinkedIn length" comes from a study of LinkedIn articles and newsletters, not feed posts. Confusing the two formats is the most common way this advice gets garbled.
- Engagement by format runs against plain text: native documents and multi image posts average well above text posts. Text is worth using because it is the cheapest format to carry an argument to a technical audience, not because the platform rewards it.

Numbers, wording and source URLs live in `references/linkedin-platform-facts.md`. Re-check them there before quoting any of it again.

## When He Hands You an Outline File

He sometimes names an `outlineN-*.txt` in the prompt instead of asking you to choose one. That file is a spec for a 1,300 to 1,600 word article, complete with `// SECTION HEADER` blocks and a WHAT TO DO TODAY checklist. Do not write the article, and do not write a post that walks the outline section by section. Compressing the outline is the job:

- The outline's opening hook is usually already the post's first line. Take it, tighten it so the claim and the attribution both survive the 140 character fold, and cut any second paragraph that only restates it.
- Two or three of the middle sections collapse into one prose block that explains the mechanism. Pick the section that teaches the reader why the thing works (the oracle explanation, the cost model), not the one that narrates the timeline. Timeline goes in a single sentence.
- Keep the action list as the numbered sequence, trimmed to what a reader can do this week. Drop items that only make sense inside a full article.
- Keep the closing question. It is already in his voice and needs no rewriting.
- **Verify every figure in the outline that you intend to use, and cut the ones the primary source labels differently.** The outline's numbers are traceable to the day's report, not to the publisher, and the report's summary line is a paraphrase. The publisher's own post is where the exact wording lives: the funnel stages, the person-months, the median and worst-case day counts, the headline ratio.
- **An outline figure that is a theme statement rather than a published number does not go in the post.** A ratio the report itself coins (discovery outrunning remediation by fifty to one) reads as a measurement to anyone who checks and cannot be traced to the publisher. Quote the measured funnel instead, and record the dropped ratios, the dropped secondary systems and the dropped corroborating stories in the report's NOT CLAIMED section with the reason for each.
- Drop every `//` header, the reading time, the category line and the cross-references to other Notes. Those belong to the article, and on LinkedIn they read as pasted blog scaffolding.

## When He Hands You a Repo or a Live Artifact

Sometimes the subject is his own work: a repo he just published, a script, a pipeline. The post then has one job, which is to prove the thing exists and does what the post says, read from the artifact itself, because a pinned post gets about eight seconds before a stranger decides to click. Read a repo through the GitHub API rather than its rendered page, map every claim to the file that implements it, and never claim adoption, scale or a team. The full shape, the `gh` commands, the report contents and the hook pattern are in `references/artifact-post-shape.md`.

## Fact-Check Discipline

- Every figure in the post gets verified against the original reporting, not against the data file. The data file is a lead. Open the outlet that broke the story and confirm the number, the attribution and the timeframe. This step has caught errors.
- The data file's own summary line is not verification. Grep the report for the story, then scrape the publisher.
- **Verify the outline's dates and its headline multiples, not only its percentages.** An outline can date a report a day off, and the publisher's own release carries the correct date, so the post quotes the publisher's date. **An outline also flattens a project's duration into the length of its most quotable stage:** a scan that ran two weeks inside a three-month effort becomes "two weeks" for the whole thing. Quote the duration the publisher labels for that stage (scan duration is not effort duration) and never imply the project ran that long. A headline multiple (a 22 times volume figure, a 60 times price ratio) is the claim most likely to have drifted in the aggregation, and the one a reader can disprove fastest.
- **When a headline multiple will not confirm at the publisher, look for that report's own equivalent metric before cutting the argument.** The primary source often measured the same thing a cleaner way, and its wording is stronger than the derived multiple: a usage share against a spend share states the same point with no arithmetic to defend. Substitute the primary metric, and record in the report which outline figure it replaced and why.
- **Attribute the claim to the entity the publisher names, not the one the aggregator implies.** A data file's summary can drop a parent company, so a product reads as belonging to the wrong firm. Read the publisher's own sentence and use that entity in the post, or use the executive's name and skip the company entirely when the product's ownership is not load bearing.
- **Carry the outlet's own hedge word into the post.** When the original says "reportedly," the post says "reportedly"; the qualifier is the difference between a sourced claim and one the reader can disprove. Note in the report which figure rests on secondary reporting rather than a first party disclosure.
- **Name a sponsor out loud in the post when the argument favours the sponsor.** A sponsored analysis inside a reputable outlet still counts as a source for its metric, but the sponsorship makes the recommendation self-interested, so the post states it in one clause and then makes the arithmetic stand on its own.
- A named framework or rubric may be his own reasoning, and usually should be. Present it as reasoning. Never attribute his rubric to the company in the story, and never put words in an executive's mouth that the reporting does not support.
- When he asks whether third-party advice is right, answer the fact-check separately from the writing. Say which part holds, which does not, and what the evidence shows. Do not soften a wrong claim into "partly right".
- No invented first-person experience. If a line would require him to have personally run something he has never described, cut it and make the point in second person.
- **Read the source's own limits section before asserting a claim.** A responsible disclosure or benchmark ends with what it did not test, and those sentences decide what the post may say. A vendor caveat such as one artifact not being retested in a later run means that artifact gets attributed to the original capture in the post, or gets dropped, never stated as current. Carrying the caveat into the report file as a NOT CLAIMED list is how the post stays defensible when someone checks it.
- Note the difference between the article's framing and what the source supports. An outline can reach for a second paper to explain a pattern; that second paper's numbers need their own verification before they appear in a post, and skipping it is cheaper than defending it.
- **When two verified figures from different sources collide numerically, cut one.** Two separate findings that both land on the same value read as a contradiction to anyone who checks, and a reader who cannot tell them apart discounts both. Keep the one that carries the argument, drop the other, and record in the report why it was cut.

## Web Research

Scrapling first, per the constitution. Run it with the Hermes venv interpreter (`~/.hermes/venv/bin/python <script>`), because the system `python3` does not have the module. `Fetcher.get(url, timeout=30)` is faster than `StealthyFetcher` and works on publisher pages.

`web_search` is discovery only: scrape the publisher URL it returns and read the actual text. Search engines are the failure point, so do not burn calls re-trying them through the fetcher, and disqualify SEO counter and character-counter pages on sight because no named human author means no citation.

**When the outlet that broke the story is paywalled or refuses the fetch, go to the primary source's own page.** A paper whose `p` nodes return one paragraph is paywalled, and syndication desks often answer 403. The organization that published the study usually has its own report page and press release, and those repeat every figure in the source's own words, which is also the correct entity to attribute. Reach for the report page first when the story is a survey, a benchmark or a funding round.

Paragraph extraction, the arXiv exception, the batch print pattern and the voice-capture recipe are in `references/web-research-notes.md`.

## Delivery

1. `SCRATCH=$(mktemp -d /tmp/hermes-linkedin-XXXXXX)`. Never a fixed path. Never `cd` into it either: the directory is deleted at the end of the run, and a shell left sitting in it fails every later command in that session with exit 126 and `cd: <dir>: No such file or directory` before the command runs. Write and run everything by absolute path and pass `workdir=~` on terminal calls, including the one that deletes the scratch dir.
2. Write the post alone to `post.txt`, and the post plus the fact-check to a dated report file (`linkedin-post-YYYY-MM-DD.txt`). Both get emailed. Build the report as its own file and copy `report.txt` to the dated name, never the reverse: copying `post.txt` over the dated name ships the bare post twice and loses the fact-check. Check the dated file's first line before attaching it. For a post about his own artifact, the dated report also names the file behind each claim and carries a NOT CLAIMED list, so anyone who opens the artifact finds the post defensible.
3. Verify before sending: `wc -w post.txt` and record the count, reporting what the draft came to rather than a pass or fail against 200 to 300, plus `wc -m` against the 3,000 character cap, which is the only real limit. Then a grep for `[\x{2013}\x{2014}]` and for ` - ` to prove no dash punctuation, and prove the report is not hard-wrapped. **The `awk` max-length test is necessary but blind to the failure that actually happens:** a report full of long paragraphs and 80 character section rules passes it comfortably while a bullet sits split across two lines. Pair it with a continuation check that flags any non-blank line ending without terminal punctuation whose next line starts with a lowercase letter, and author every report paragraph and bullet as ONE unwrapped line to begin with. When repair is needed, unwrap with a targeted edit per bullet: a blanket join of consecutive indented lines glues an entire bullet list into one paragraph and buries the section that follows it. Also measure the hook: print the length of each of the first three physical lines and their combined length, and confirm the third line is a complete sentence rather than mid-clause; a short combined total does not prove the shape when the first physical line is itself 135 characters, so check the individual lengths. It renders as the three lines a phone shows before See More, so it has to be three short physical lines, not one long line that wraps. A first draft clears the fold on screen by luck, not by design, so quote the number. **Count emphasis beats by length, not by whether a block is one physical line.** A paragraph authored as one unwrapped line of 200 characters counts as a "single-line block" in a naive split-on-blank-line measurement, which makes a correctly shaped post report as bro-etry. Print each block's character length and sentence count instead, flag any prose block over three sentences and split it at the joint where the idea changes, and count a block as an emphasis beat only when it is short, under roughly 120 characters. Then the report can state both numbers honestly: prose blocks of two to three sentences, and a small number of short beats. **Fill the report's MEASUREMENTS block last, from the file as it stands when the text is final.** Numbers typed ahead of the last wording edit are the most common way a report contradicts the attachment it was emailed with.
4. Spell check both with pyspellchecker in a venv, passing a LIST of words to `spell.unknown(...)` and sanity-checking the checker against `"teh receve"` first. Expect proper nouns, brands and jargon to come back; verify those by hand. `rollout`, `LLM`, `GPUs`, `LinkedIn` and author surnames flag every time. British spellings (`behaviour`, `licence`, `organisation`) do not flag because they are real words, and they are wrong for a US audience: grep the draft for `our` and `ise` endings and convert them.
5. Email through the `email-sender` skill, TO and FROM `hello@phantom-byte.com`. Subject short enough not to fold, under roughly 60 characters.
6. Verify delivery by reading the INBOX copy back with `scripts/verify_recent_headers.py --contains "<fragment>"` and confirming the attachment filename. Report only what it printed.
7. Telegram notice is a doorbell: what was sent, the subject, and the one-line fact-check verdict. Do not paste the post into chat.

## Revisions

He revises by downloading the emailed file and sending back a change list, each item with the reason behind it. Treat every item as an order, not a suggestion.

- **A note that praises a property is an instruction about that property, never a certificate that the draft is fine.** He forwards an outside review and says to apply it. When an item reads as approval ("the opening three lines are punchy and data-dense", "the numbered list makes it easy to digest"), the work is to raise that property one notch and prove the improvement with a measure: give the hook the actor it is missing, split a block at its joint, trim words out of a list item, cut filler phrasing. Answering a revision round with an unchanged file is the outcome he rejects outright, and it does not become acceptable because the notes happened to name no defect. If one item genuinely cannot improve, improve the property the same section names and state in WHAT CHANGED what changed for it. Never email a byte-identical post back and call it applied.
- **Ownership and engagement items.** A review that asks which parts are his gets a boundary drawn in the post, never an invented credential: one sentence saying this is research with no product shipping it, one saying "I am not claiming their benchmark, I am claiming the pattern under it," and the framework itself in first person ("the rule I would put on the wall"). That answers the hiring manager without claiming a benchmark he never ran. An engagement item is a SECOND question placed after the closing challenge and above the hashtags, offering pickable answers (per task, per adapter, per tenant) so an engineer can reply in one line instead of composing a paragraph; the closing challenge stays, it is not replaced by the prompt.
- Apply each item literally and completely. "Tighten this noun" means find every instance of the vague phrasing, not the first one.
- The core survives unless he says to change it: the thesis, the verified statistic, and the closing call to action stay untouched. Reformatting is not a licence to rewrite the argument.
- Every original constraint still holds after the edit. Reformatting into lists changes the word count, so re-run `wc -w` and the dash grep and the spell check on the revised text; a list conversion has pushed a draft past the ceiling before.
- Add a WHAT CHANGED section to the delivered file that names each instruction and what changed for it, so he can check the work against his own list in one pass.
- Name the file `<base>-revised.txt` and put "Revised" in the subject. A revised copy must not be mistakable in the inbox for the draft it replaces, and the IMAP read-back is matched on that subject.
- The Telegram notice reports what changed in two or three lines. Still no paste of the post.

### Formatting revision items he sends

He forwards platform-optimization notes and expects them applied as written, not judged. Three of them recur, and each has a mechanical check so the reply can state a number instead of an impression.

- **Whitespace and rhythm.** Two settings, and the second is the one he asks for after the first overshoots. More whitespace means a break between IDEAS: split any block carrying three or more sentences, and split a long explanation at its joints. It does not mean putting every full stop on its own line, which is the bro-etry shape he rejects; the target is two to three sentence paragraphs with a handful of single-line beats for emphasis. Verify by counting, not by eye: no prose block over three sentences, and single-line blocks a clear minority of the total. Report both counts.
- **Hook above the fold.** A phone shows THREE lines of feed before See More, so the hook is the first two short non-blank lines adding up to roughly 130 characters: measure them together, leave margin under 140, and count only non-blank lines because the blank line between the hook and its qualifier still consumes characters in the rendered post. Do the arithmetic in python over `[x for x in text.split('\n') if x.strip()]` and print the combined length, then quote that number in the report. Aim for two short lines rather than one long one, so the third rendered line ends on a complete sentence instead of slicing the first body paragraph mid-clause. **When the first body paragraph is a single physical line of 250 characters or more, which is the normal case for a post built from an outline, two short hook lines still fail:** the third rendered line becomes the opening of that long paragraph and slices it anyway, whatever the first two lines add up to. Then promote the hook to three short lines, the third being a complete sentence of its own that carries the number the post is about, and measure all three together under about 135 characters. Check the third rendered line directly, printing its text and length, instead of trusting the two-line arithmetic to imply it. **Author the hook on its own physical lines, one sentence per line.** A hook typed as one 135 character line passes the combined-length arithmetic while rendering as a wrapped paragraph whose third line slices mid-sentence, so the sum proves nothing about what the reader sees. Print each of the first three physical lines with its own length next to the total, where the total is roughly 130 to 135 and no single line exceeds about 70; a first-line length anywhere near the total means the hook is still one long line. When the pair overflows, shorten the first line and rewrite the qualifier to say the same thing in fewer words; do not delete the qualifier, since it is often what keeps the claim honest (a retail value disclosure is not cash spend).
- **Hashtags.** 3 to 4 on the last line, after the closing question, drawn from the tags he names when he names them, otherwise the audience's tags.

**When he asks why the hook looks the way it does.** Answer with the arithmetic, not the aesthetics: give each of the three line lengths and the combined total against the roughly 140 character fold, and say what the fold would slice if the hook were written as one normal paragraph. Then name the real cost in the same answer, that a mechanical rule produces the same stacked-fragment shape whatever the topic, because that repetition is the thing he is actually noticing. He asks before editing precisely because it looks like a defect, so a vague "it reads better on mobile" is what makes him fix it himself. Do not reformat the file to answer a question, and do not apologize for the shape: it is load bearing, and an unrequested change moves the number the post is about onto the wrong side of the fold without altering a word he can see.

Applying these changes the character and word counts and can push a block count up, so re-run the `wc -w`, `wc -m`, dash grep and spell check afterward and restate all of them in the report. The report's own numbers must match the file that is attached: patch the copy first, then re-measure, then write the numbers down.

## Pitfalls

- Do not paste the finished post into Telegram. He asked for email, and the notification is not the delivery.
- Do not pick a topic because it is trending. Pick the one that lets a hiring manager see how he reasons.
- Do not let the framework be the entire post. A rubric with no evidence behind it is a listicle. One verified number before the rubric, one after.
- Do not re-run the sender after a clean send. The IMAP read-back is the verification, not a second email.
- Do not answer the fact-check from memory of what the platforms say. The whole reason this skill exists is that the popular 200 to 300 word claim is fabricated and gets repeated unless it is checked.
