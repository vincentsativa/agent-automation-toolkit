# LinkedIn platform facts, verified

Re-check these before quoting them in a post or in a fact-check answer. Every entry names the source and what that source actually says, so a future session can tell a real number from a repeated one.

## Length

- "A LinkedIn post must be 200 to 300 words": FALSE, and it is this skill's own error to avoid. Nothing requires a word count. The 200 to 300 range is a house starting point for a single-claim post, not a rule. A post carrying a numbered sequence and a bulleted evidence list lands around 290 to 330, and a single-point post can land at 150. Both are fine, and neither gets reported as a hit or a miss.
- "LinkedIn recommends 200 to 300 words per post": FALSE. No LinkedIn published source recommends a word count. The figure circulates in third-party marketing blogs that cite each other.
- "3,000 character cap on a feed post": TRUE. LinkedIn help, and Hootsuite's post-length guide: "The LinkedIn post length limit is 3,000 characters, but your text gets cut off behind a See More button after around 140 characters."
- "Keep the opening under about 150 characters": third-party recommendation. Hootsuite ideal-length guide, LinkedIn section: "Organic and paid LinkedIn post length: under 150 characters above the fold."
- "1,900 to 2,000 words is the ideal LinkedIn length": TRUE but the wrong format. Hootsuite guide, citing Paul Shapiro of Search Wilderness, an analysis of more than 3,000 top-performing posts on LinkedIn's publishing platform. That is articles and newsletters, not feed posts.

A 200 to 300 word post lands around 1,300 to 1,800 characters, so it fits under the cap comfortably. Fitting is not the same as recommended.

## Ranking

LinkedIn Engineering, "Engineering the next generation of LinkedIn's Feed," authored by Hristo Danchev, March 12, 2026. URL: https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/engineering-the-next-generation-of-linkedins-feed

What the post states: the feed has long been AI-powered; the new system is a unified retrieval layer built on fine-tuned LLM embeddings plus a sequential ranking model that learns from how members move through content over time, including what they read, like, comment on, return to, or scroll past. It does not publish a word count, a format preference, or a posting-time rule.

Use it only to support the narrow claim that ranking is semantic and behavioural rather than length based.

## Engagement by format

Socialinsider, LinkedIn Organic Benchmarks 2026, by Elena Cucu, built on 1.3 million LinkedIn business posts. URL: https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/linkedin-post-length/ (serves the benchmarks page).

Average engagement rate by format, Q3 2026: native documents 6.40 percent, multi image 6.40 percent, video 5.25 percent, image 4.70 percent, polls 3.60 percent, text 3.50 percent, link posts 2.50 percent.

Read this as format engagement, not as a length finding. It is the honest counterweight to any claim that a plain text post is the platform's best performing format.

## The fold and list formatting

The feed shows roughly the first 140 characters before See More, so anything below that line is a click away, not free. Two consequences for structure:

- The claim line has to land inside the fold on its own. A numbered list that starts below the fold is invisible to a skimmer who never expands.
- A list or bullet block placed early is the cheapest way to survive the fold with a technical audience, because it reads as a skeleton rather than as prose. This is a legibility argument, not a ranking claim: no LinkedIn source says lists rank better.

A 291 word post with a four item numbered list and three bullets measured 1,702 characters, so list formatting costs characters but leaves plenty of room under the 3,000 cap.

## Credibility screen for length advice

Almost every page ranking for LinkedIn post length is an SEO counter or scheduler tool built to capture the search. Disqualify a source when it has no named human author, when it discloses AI drafting, or when its only citation is another post of the same kind. What survives: LinkedIn's own help and engineering posts, and established trade publishers with named writers who describe their dataset (Hootsuite, Buffer, Socialinsider).
